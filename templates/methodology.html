SECTION 01 Data Governance

1. Data Trust Score Formula & Weighted Components

Rather than relying on vague qualitative assessments, WeatherWise scores raw meteorological datasets using a transparent 0-to-100 deterministic audit index:

Trust Score = (w₁ × S_missing) + (w₂ × S_duplicates) + (w₃ × S_validity) + (w₄ × S_continuity)

Where weights are strictly normalized (Σ wᵢ = 1.0):
• w₁ = 0.30   (Missing values penalty based on total cell missingness rate)
• w₂ = 0.20   (Duplicate chronological timestamp penalties)
• w₃ = 0.25   (Physical domain boundary violations, e.g. Temp > 60°C or Rain < 0)
• w₄ = 0.25   (Date sequence continuity and gap interval frequency)

A score ≥ 90 indicates High Trust suitable for statistical modeling; 75–89 denotes Moderate Trust requiring targeted median imputation; and < 75 denotes Low Trust data requiring sensor recalibration.

SECTION 02 Outlier Statistics

2. Interquartile Range (IQR) Anomaly Detection

The IQR method (pioneered by John Tukey) is a non-parametric outlier detection protocol. Unlike standard deviation, the IQR is resistant to extreme outliers because it focuses solely on the middle 50% of ranked observations.

Q₁ = 25th Percentile   |   Q₃ = 75th Percentile
IQR = Q₃ - Q₁

Lower Outlier Fence = Q₁ - (k × IQR)
Upper Outlier Fence = Q₃ + (k × IQR)

[Default Sensitivity Multiplier: k = 1.5 for mild outliers; k = 3.0 for extreme outliers]

Any daily weather observation falling strictly outside the interval [Lower Fence, Upper Fence] is highlighted as a candidate anomaly in Weather Detective.

SECTION 03 Parametric Deviation

3. Standardized Z-Score & Gaussian Assumptions

The Z-score represents how many standard deviations (σ) a daily measurement diverges from the overall sample arithmetic mean (μ):

Z = (x - μ) / σ

Condition for Flagging: |Z| ≥ 3.0   (or custom threshold k σ)

Caveat: Z-scores presume a roughly symmetrical Gaussian distribution. For highly skewed metrics like rainfall (which is zero-inflated and exponential), the IQR fence or rolling window is strongly preferred.

SECTION 04 Contextual Outliers

4. Rolling Moving-Window Anomaly Detection

Global anomaly detectors miss seasonal anomalies: for example, a temperature of 24°C is completely normal in July, but represents an extreme outlier if observed in mid-January. Rolling window detection evaluates each point strictly against its local temporal neighborhood:

For observation at time t and window span W (e.g. 30 days):
μ_{t, W} = (1 / W) × Σ xᵢ     [for i ∈ (t - W/2, t + W/2)]
σ_{t, W} = √ [ (1 / W) × Σ (xᵢ - μ_{t,W})² ]

Flag if: |x_t - μ_{t, W}| > (threshold × σ_{t, W})
SECTION 05 Forecasting Baselines

5. Holt-Winters Exponential Smoothing

Exponential smoothing recursively calculates weighted running estimates of the level ($L_t$), slope trend ($b_t$), and recurring seasonal components ($S_t$), giving exponentially decreasing weight to older historical measurements:

Level Update:     L_t = α(Y_t - S_{t-m}) + (1 - α)(L_{t-1} + b_{t-1})
Trend Update:     b_t = β(L_t - L_{t-1}) + (1 - β)b_{t-1}
Season Update:   S_t = γ(Y_t - L_{t-1} - b_{t-1}) + (1 - γ)S_{t-m}

h-step Ahead Forecast:   Ŷ_{t+h} = L_t + h × b_t + S_{t+h-m}

Limitations: Exponential smoothing models mean trajectory and periodic sine waves; it cannot anticipate chaotic non-linear shifts such as cold front arrivals or cyclonic squalls.

SECTION 06 Component Breakdown

6. Classical Seasonal Decomposition (Additive vs Multiplicative)

Separates observed chronological weather series into three distinct mathematical components:

Additive Model (Constant Seasonal Amplitude):
Y(t) = Trend(t) + Seasonal(t) + Residual(t)

Multiplicative Model (Seasonal Amplitude Proportional to Level):
Y(t) = Trend(t) × Seasonal(t) × Residual(t)

The Trend represents the smoothed multi-year climatic trajectory; Seasonal captures repeating annual solar cycles; and Residual isolates random meteorological turbulence.

SECTION 07 Bivariate Associations

7. Pearson Linear vs Spearman Monotonic Correlation

WeatherWise computes both Pearson and Spearman correlation matrices:

Pearson (r): Measures strict linear co-variance between continuous variables.
r = Σ((x - x̄)(y - ȳ)) / [ √Σ(x - x̄)² × √Σ(y - ȳ)² ]

Spearman (ρ): Measures monotonic association between rank-ordered data.
ρ = 1 - [ (6 × Σ dᵢ²) / (n(n² - 1)) ]   [where dᵢ = rank(xᵢ) - rank(yᵢ)]

Use Pearson for continuous normally distributed pairings (e.g. Temperature vs Pressure). Use Spearman when relationships are non-linear or contain extreme spikes (e.g. Rainfall vs Wind Speed).

SECTION 08 Model Verification

8. Out-of-Sample Chronological Validation & Metrics

In time series analysis, standard random K-Fold cross-validation constitutes a cardinal data leakage sin. WeatherWise splits datasets strictly chronologically:

Chronological Split: Training set = Days 1 to (N × (1 - ratio))   |   Test set = Days (N × (1 - ratio) + 1) to N

Mean Absolute Error (MAE):   MAE = (1 / n) × Σ |y_actual - y_pred|
Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE):   RMSE = √ [ (1 / n) × Σ (y_actual - y_pred)² ]

RMSE penalizes large, jarring prediction errors more heavily than MAE, making it the preferred benchmark for municipal storm preparedness.

SECTION 10 Epistemic Boundaries

10. Scientific Scope: What WeatherWise Can and Cannot Claim

What WeatherWise Provides:
  • Deterministic, auditable statistical outlier detection.
  • Mathematical time-series decompositions and seasonal amplitudes.
  • Unbiased out-of-sample forecast model bakeoffs.
  • Standardized multi-pillar data quality grading and gap auditing.
What WeatherWise Does NOT Claim:
  • Does not provide official governmental weather safety warnings.
  • Does not execute 3D Navier-Stokes atmospheric fluid models.
